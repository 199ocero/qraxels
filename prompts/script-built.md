The viewer should finish the video able to explain to a friend HOW this thing was built or how it worked: the actual method, step by step, not just that it's impressive. Think of a smart friend with a sketchbook: curious, clear, a little amazed, never sensational.

No research: write from what you already know. Before writing, work out (don't output this separately):
- THE QUESTION: the one thing that makes this hard to believe (the weight, the size, the tools they didn't have, the time it took).
- THE METHOD: how it was actually done, as a chain of cause and effect: the trick, the tool or the principle that made it possible. This is the heart of the video.
- THE PICTURE: one familiar comparison that makes the scale or the method obvious (a block as heavy as two cars, a ramp like a mountain switchback road). Set it up, return to it, pay it off at the end.
- THE PIECE: one concrete unit followed through (one block, one day on site, one arch, one gear turning).
- THE STEPS: the build or the working in 3–4 ordered steps.
- THE EVIDENCE AND THE LIMIT: how we know (a surviving tool, a papyrus, marks on the stone, an experiment), and what historians still debate or a popular myth that isn't true.

Tag every beat with its section, in this order:
1. hook (1–2 lines): the question, in the first three seconds, as a specific, concrete puzzle ("Two and a half tonnes. No wheels. No iron. So how?"). Never open with "Have you ever wondered" or the date.
2. context (2–3 beats): what it is, who built it, roughly when, and the size of the problem, in plain words. Set up the picture here.
3. idea (2–4 beats): the method, named and explained mechanism-first. "Here's the trick." This is where the music comes in.
4. example (1–3 beats): the piece followed through, so the method is obvious.
5. how (2–4 beats): the build or the working in order. Say the steps out loud ("First... Then... Finally...").
6. caveat (1–2 beats): how we know, and what's still debated or the myth that isn't true. Honest, specific.
7. close (1–2 beats): return to the hook or the picture, now understood. The last line is the one they'll remember, and it ends with a full stop.

Clarity rules (the most important part):
- Every line follows from the line before it. Never mention something that hasn't been set up.
- Define every technical term the first time, in plain words (a lintel is the stone laid across the top).
- Plain English a 12-year-old could follow. Sentences of 6–20 words. One idea per sentence.
- Signpost: "Here's the problem.", "Here's the trick.", "So how do we know?"
- Say "you" and put the viewer on site ("Imagine you're hauling this up the ramp."). Calm, curious. No "!" except at most once.
- Before you finish, read it as someone who has never heard of this place or machine. If any line would make them ask "wait, what?", rewrite it.

Accuracy rules (the channel lives on trust):
- Up to 4 facts, numbers, dates or named sources, only ones that are well established; list every one in flagged_claims. Round honestly ("about 2.3 million blocks", "around 2560 BC").
- Where historians disagree, say so in plain words ("most archaeologists think...", "one theory is..."). Never present a theory as settled.
- No aliens, lost-civilisation or "they couldn't have done it" claims, except to show the evidence against them. No made-up quotes or studies.
- No hype vocabulary: "mind-blowing", "insane", "you won't believe", "secret they don't want you to know".
- Each beat = 1–3 sentences + a one-line idea of what's on screen. Think in OBJECTS from this topic: the structure, a cross-section of it, the tools (ramp, sledge, lever, pulley, crane, wedge), a timeline of dates, the people at work, the ruin today.
- visual_idea: every scene is drawn fresh for this video, so describe a picture only THIS line could have: the objects, where the hooded character is and what they're doing, and the one thing that moves or changes ("the needle of a friction gauge drops from 10 to 5 as water hits the sand"). Bold black-and-white line art: shapes, silhouettes, diagrams, arrows; no maps of many places, crowds of detail or long text.
- No two beats share a picture. When the close returns to the hook's picture, show it changed (now finished, now understood), not a copy.
- HARD LIMIT: 210–270 words of narration in total. Count them. It's read calmly, about 2.25 words a second with the pauses, so this fills 95–120 seconds. Over 270 runs past two minutes.

Field meanings for this style:
- title: the question, clear and specific (e.g. "How did they lift a 30-tonne stone with no cranes?").
- analogy: the picture, in a few words.
- analogy_breaks_down: what's still debated or uncertain; it's what the caveat says.
- flagged_claims: every fact, number, date or named source you used, so the user can double-check it.
